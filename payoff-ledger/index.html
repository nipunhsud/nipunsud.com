Pay off early or invest?

Compares two versions of your plan. In the first, every debt gets extra payments. In the second, debts below the cutoff rate get only their minimums, and that money goes to your emergency fund and then to investing or retirement. Both use the same money each month.

PlanDebt leftCash savingsInvestedInterest paidNet worth

Paying off a debt earns its interest rate, guaranteed. Investing only earns its expected return on average, and some years it loses money. If your employer matches retirement contributions, the match is an instant 50–100% return, so take the full match first no matter what (count it in Essentials). Pre-tax retirement contributions also cut your tax bill, which this comparison leaves out, so it slightly undersells retirement.

Why this method works

  1. Build a starter buffer first. Most payoff plans fail because a surprise bill lands on a credit card. A small cash buffer stops that cycle.
  2. Always pay every minimum. Missed minimums bring fees, penalty rates and credit damage that erase any progress.
  3. Throw every spare dollar at one debt at a time. Avalanche targets the highest rate and costs the least. Snowball targets the smallest balance and gives quicker wins. The plan shows the cost difference, so you can pick knowingly.
  4. Keep saving a little the whole way. A fixed slice of your extra money goes to savings. It slows payoff slightly but keeps the buffer growing, which is what makes the plan survive a bad month.
  5. Roll payments forward. When a debt is gone, its whole payment moves to the next one. Your monthly outlay stays the same while the attack gets bigger.
  6. After the last debt, redirect everything to savings until you hold your emergency fund goal. Then keep the same habit for investing.

If your employer matches retirement contributions, contribute enough to get the full match before extra debt payments; count that as an essential here. Rates above about 7% usually beat what savings or investments earn, so they come first.
